# Reflection
## PawGuide AI — Evaluation Design Challenges
### Step 6: Three Reflection Questions

---

## Question 1: What would change in your evaluation design if your client's data was in French?

Expanding PawGuide AI into France — a natural V3 market — would require fundamental changes to the evaluation design rather than simple translation. The most immediate challenge is benchmark availability: HealthBench covers only 3.5% French-language conversations, the Veterinary Undergraduate MCQ benchmark is English-only, and TruthfulQA has no validated French adaptation. This means the custom evaluation suite would need to be fully redesigned in French from scratch, including new prompt wording, new rule-based checks for French emergency terminology (such as "immédiatement" and "urgence"), and new RAG source material drawn from French veterinary literature rather than German curriculum materials. Dr. Lund's review capacity would not extend to French-language outputs, requiring identification of a French veterinary advisor for calibration — a significant resourcing challenge.

Beyond translation, French presents specific linguistic challenges that affect evaluation quality. French veterinary and medical terminology differs meaningfully from German equivalents, and the LLM-as-judge — calibrated on German outputs — would require full recalibration for French to avoid penalizing culturally appropriate French phrasing patterns. Research has shown that LLM performance on medical benchmarks drops approximately 10 percentage points for French compared to English, meaning the baseline model performance assumptions built into the current evaluation design would need to be revised downward before setting pass/fail thresholds.

A French evaluation would also need to address French-specific regulatory and cultural context. French pet owners interact with the veterinary system differently from German owners — consultation patterns, prescription culture, and trust in digital health tools all differ. The judge's criteria for what constitutes an "appropriate" response would need to be recalibrated to reflect French veterinary practice norms rather than German ones. In short, French is not a translation problem — it is a full evaluation redesign problem that requires a new advisor, new source corpus, new judge calibration, and revised performance baselines.

## Question 2: Your client asks "is this model AGI-level?" — how do you respond?

The honest answer is that this question cannot be meaningfully answered in the context of PawGuide AI, and attempting to answer it would be professionally irresponsible. AGI — Artificial General Intelligence — implies human-level performance across all cognitive domains. PawGuide is a narrow, domain-specific application evaluated on five carefully scoped veterinary advisory tasks. Strong performance within that narrow scope says nothing about general intelligence. A model that scores 5 out of 5 on all five PawGuide evaluation prompts has demonstrated competence at pre-consultation pet health advisory in German — nothing more, nothing less.

Evaluations that would actually be needed to approach an AGI-level claim would be vastly more extensive: multi-domain reasoning across all major fields of human knowledge, open-ended problem solving in novel situations never seen in training, causal reasoning, long-horizon planning, common sense understanding, and consistent performance across all human languages and cultural contexts. No current benchmark comes close to testing all of these dimensions comprehensively, and the ones that attempt broad coverage — such as BIG-Bench Hard or ARC-AGI — still represent only a narrow slice of what genuine general intelligence would require.

What the PawGuide evaluation does allow us to say is whether the model performs at a level appropriate for this specific task under these specific conditions. Even that more modest claim carries important caveats: our evaluation suite covers five prompts, not the full distribution of real production queries; our RAG corpus reflects one German veterinary institution's curriculum; and our judge calibration is based on Dr. Lund's expert review of a limited reference set. These are meaningful but bounded findings. Any investor or partner asking whether the model is "AGI-level" should be redirected toward the specific, honest findings in this evaluation rather than an unanswerable general claim. The appropriate response is: "Under these conditions, for this task, the model performs strongly — that is what we can responsibly say."

## Question 3: What is the one thing you could not evaluate without a human, and why?

The one evaluation dimension that cannot be automated — not by rule-based checks, not by LLM-as-judge, not by RAG faithfulness scoring — is the clinical judgment of whether a response's overall framing is genuinely safe for a frightened, non-expert pet owner in a real emergency situation. Rule-based checks can confirm that the word "immediately" appears in a response. The LLM-as-judge can score whether the tone seems appropriately urgent. RAG faithfulness scoring can verify that medical claims are grounded in veterinary literature. But none of these automated methods can replicate what Dr. Lund does when she reads a response and asks: "Would I be comfortable if a worried owner in Germany at 11pm read this and acted on it?" That judgment integrates clinical knowledge, understanding of owner psychology, awareness of what information gets misread under stress, and professional accountability — none of which an automated system currently possesses.

This matters most for edge cases that are technically correct but practically dangerous — a response that mentions GDV in the third paragraph after two paragraphs of reassurance, or a response that lists aortic thromboembolism as one of six bullet points without conveying that it is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate action. An LLM judge may score these responses as passing because the information is present and grounded in the RAG. Dr. Lund would flag them because a panicking pet owner at midnight would not process a buried bullet point as a call to emergency action. That gap between technical correctness and practical safety is irreducibly human.

In practice, this human evaluation is incorporated through Dr. Lund's quarterly calibration reviews, where she assesses a targeted sample of flagged responses — specifically the lowest-scoring and highest-variance outputs from each evaluation cycle. Additionally, veterinary working students (Werkstudenten) at German university veterinary programs can serve as a cost-effective first-pass human review layer for routine outputs, escalating ambiguous cases to Dr. Lund. This tiered approach keeps Dr. Lund's time commitment realistic — under one hour per quarter — while ensuring her clinical judgment remains the ultimate authority on the evaluation pipeline's reliability. No deployment decision should be made without her explicit sign-off on the safety-critical prompt category, regardless of how well automated evaluation scores perform.
